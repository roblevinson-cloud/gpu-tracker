Hardware value

Median Vast.ai $/GPU-hr normalized by hardware specs · editable in gpu_specs.yml.
There is no single "price of compute." The solid lines use dense FP16/BF16 — the only tensor format present and full-rate on all five vintages, and the only one monotonic with age (312→989→989→2250→2250). Dashed lines use each part's best deployable precision (FP4 on Blackwell, FP8 on Hopper, FP16 on Ampere); the gap between them is the quantisation dividend. Change the denominator and the ranking inverts, and the age chart's slope flips sign — which is why its headline stays suppressed.
Why not INT8 or INT4? INT4 exists only on A100: Hopper dropped it and Blackwell never restored it (its 4-bit formats are FP4, floating point). INT8 is on all five but is non-monotonic — NVIDIA cut HGX B300 INT8 to 3 POPS against B200's 72, so a 2025 B300 rates below a 2020 A100. Normalising by INT8 would rank the newest silicon worst, which is an artifact, not a price signal. (GPTQ/AWQ "INT4" is W4A16 — a storage format that dequantises to float, so it belongs on the memory axis, not a compute one.)
Read the age chart as a cross-section, not a depreciation curve. Every vintage has only weeks of price history, so it compares four generations at one moment rather than tracking any of them as it ages. Vertical position is driven by scarcity (see utilization), not age. B300's fleet is only ~48 GPUs — roughly 39% volatility over three weeks — so treat its line as indicative at best.
Memory and bandwidth don't move with the precision choice at all — a byte is a byte — which is why they stay the robust axes. Memory is measured on usable capacity, not nameplate: NVIDIA's own DGX B200 page gives 1,440 GB across 8 GPUs (180/GPU against a 192 nameplate), and Vast reports the same 180.
A100 is tracked but deliberately held out of these four cross-vintage charts. It trades as a different market — trailing edge, no FP8 or FP4, priced off bandwidth rather than compute — so including it widened every spread and weakened the age fit without changing any conclusion. Its prices, supply, utilisation and own price trend are all still on this page. Reading it separately: A100 is the most expensive card per unit of compute and the cheapest per GB and per TB/s, which is why a 2020 part still runs ~70% rented.
The within-vintage chart is the one that can actually answer the depreciation question. Age and architecture are perfectly collinear across vintages — every H100 is both the oldest and Hopper — so no cross-section can separate ageing from newer silicon at any precision. Tracking each vintage against itself holds generation constant. A rate appears only when its 2σ interval fits inside ±15pp; until then the panel counts down, because annualising a three-week drift multiplies noise by ~17× and produces figures like −1142%/yr. H200 qualifies first.
Early hint from the one controlled pair available today: H100 and H200 are the same silicon with identical FP8 FLOPs, 18 months apart, and they price within 2% of each other per GB (2.77¢ vs 2.72¢) — leaving almost nothing for age. Suggestive, not settled: two points, three weeks.
